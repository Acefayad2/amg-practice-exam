01 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Qualified describes a legal status

Elena's employer wants to establish a qualified retirement plan. Qualified does not mean the employer bought a particular insurance product or promised the highest investment return. The plan must satisfy applicable tax-law requirements in both its written terms and actual operation. Its assets must serve the exclusive benefit of participating employees and their beneficiaries, rather than being diverted for the employer's unrelated use. Coverage, contributions, benefits, vesting and distribution provisions must satisfy the relevant rules. An owner cannot simply label a personal investment a qualified plan and claim the same treatment. Start with the legal arrangement and the people it must cover, then identify the investments used inside it.

02 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Nondiscrimination does not mean identical dollar deposits

A qualified plan generally must satisfy rules against discrimination in favor of highly compensated employees. This is different from requiring every worker to receive exactly the same dollar amount. A permitted formula can use compensation and other allowed factors, and specific plan designs have different testing or safe-harbor requirements. The plan must also follow applicable eligibility and participation rules. An employer should not assume that part-time workers can always be ignored. Compare the actual workforce with the plan's current legal requirements. On the exam, a proposal to benefit only a favored owner while improperly excluding eligible employees raises a qualification problem, even if the employer calls the arrangement a retirement benefit.

03 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Vesting means ownership, not immediate spending access

The employee has five thousand dollars attributable to her own salary deferrals and four thousand dollars attributable to employer contributions. Her own deferrals are fully vested. Assume the plan's lawful schedule makes sixty percent of that employer portion vested today, with no other adjustments. She owns all five thousand dollars of her deferrals plus twenty-four hundred dollars of the employer portion, for seventy-four hundred dollars of vested value. Vesting schedules cannot take away her own salary deferrals. Also separate ownership from access: being fully vested does not necessarily authorize a withdrawal at any moment. The plan's distribution rules, tax consequences and any applicable contract charges still need separate review.

04 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Employer and employee advantages are different

An employer can generally deduct qualifying plan contributions within the applicable limits. A deduction reduces the income used to calculate tax; it is not automatically a dollar-for-dollar reduction of the employer's tax bill. For an employee making an allowed pretax salary deferral, that amount generally is excluded from current federal income-tax wages. It normally remains subject to applicable Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes. Investment growth generally accumulates without current annual income tax inside the qualified arrangement. Designated Roth contributions work differently: they are included in current income, with qualified later distributions potentially excluded. Do not teach that every contribution or every eventual benefit is tax-free merely because the plan is qualified.

05 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

A distribution has both income and additional-tax questions

Suppose David receives a ten thousand dollar cash distribution from a qualified plan containing only pretax amounts and untaxed earnings. No rollover occurs. The full ten thousand dollars generally is ordinary taxable income. If he is forty-five and no additional-tax exception applies, another one thousand dollars of federal tax generally results from the ten percent early-distribution rule. Reaching age fifty-nine and one half removes that ordinary age-based additional tax, but does not automatically remove income tax on pretax money. Some exceptions apply earlier. For example, a covered employer-plan distribution after separation during or after the year the worker turns fifty-five can qualify for an exception. That particular separation exception does not apply to an IRA.

06 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Required distributions and retirement dates are not identical

For traditional pretax benefits, required minimum distributions depend on the applicable age, plan and retirement facts. Consider an employee reaching age seventy-three in 2026 who still works for the sponsoring employer. If the employee is not a five-percent owner and the plan permits the applicable delay, the required beginning date can wait until retirement. Do not carry that still-working rule into a traditional IRA or assume it postpones every former-employer account. Designated Roth plan accounts no longer have owner lifetime required minimum distributions under the current rule, although beneficiary requirements remain. Keep four questions separate: does the plan qualify, what does the employee own, when may money leave, and how is that payment taxed?

APPLY / IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Go one step deeper.

Eligibility depends on the current rules

Do not memorize a blanket rule that every part-time worker is excluded or that all plans have identical eligibility. Applicable age/service, long-term part-time and plan-specific provisions matter. Nondiscrimination can allow a lawful percentage-of-compensation formula rather than equal dollars.

Example: Qualified status is a condition of the arrangement, not a guarantee of investment performance.

Employee deferrals and employer vesting are separate

Employee salary deferrals are always fully vested. A qualified defined-contribution plan can use a lawful employer vesting schedule, including the usual three-year cliff or six-year graded schedules, or faster vesting. SEP and SIMPLE IRA contributions are always fully vested. Normal-retirement-age and plan-termination requirements also matter.

Example: The video’s stated 60% employer vesting produces $5,000 + $2,400 = $7,400; it is not a 60% reduction of the whole account.

The separation exception belongs to particular employer-plan payments

The age-55 exception generally requires separation during or after the calendar year the employee reaches 55. An earlier separation followed by simply reaching 55 is not the same event. Special public-safety categories have different provisions. The exception does not automatically travel into an IRA rollover.

Example: Additional-tax relief does not erase ordinary tax on pretax distributions.

Use current required-distribution rules

The age-73 example concerns someone reaching that age in 2026, not every birth cohort. Current law schedules age 75 for younger cohorts. A permitted still-working delay concerns the sponsoring employer’s plan and an employee who is not a 5% owner. Roth designated accounts have no owner lifetime RMD requirement from 2024, while beneficiary rules remain.

Example: Reaching retirement age does not force every worker to surrender an annuity.
