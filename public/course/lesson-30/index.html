01 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

One family can have several insured benefits

Marcus and Elena are reviewing protection for themselves and their children. A family plan can combine a principal insured's base coverage with separate spouse or child coverage through riders. A common arrangement combines permanent coverage on one adult with term coverage on eligible family members. The actual contract determines amounts, eligibility, expiration, and any conversion rights. Under a child life rider, the insured child's covered death triggers the benefit. The adult owner's death is a different event. Listing children as beneficiaries on an adult's policy is different from insuring their lives. Begin by identifying every insured person and each separate benefit.

02 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

First-death joint life pays at the first covered death

In a simplified joint first-death contract, Marcus and Elena are both insured under one policy. It pays one stated death benefit when the first insured dies while coverage is in force. Assume there are no additional riders or continuation provisions. After that payment, this base coverage does not promise another benefit when the surviving insured later dies. This design can address a need that arises at the first death, such as replacing lost household income. It differs from two separate individual policies, each of which can have its own death claim. Count the promised benefits instead of counting only the insured names.

03 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Survivorship coverage waits for the last insured death

Now replace that contract with a last-survivor policy, often called second-to-die coverage for two insured lives. Its base death benefit becomes payable after the second insured dies while the contract remains in force. It can address a need expected after both parents are gone, such as a legacy or money for estate obligations. It does not, by itself, replace income for the surviving spouse at the first death. Additional riders can change a real contract's benefits, so keep the example's assumptions explicit. The first-death and second-death designs answer different timing needs even though both name two insured people.

04 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Juvenile life insures the child’s life

Elena owns a juvenile whole life policy on her daughter. Elena is the owner, her daughter is the insured, and the named beneficiary receives the covered death benefit. The policy does not insure Elena's life merely because she pays. Ownership can later be transferred through the applicable process, but adulthood alone does not prove that every contract automatically transfers it. A student or juvenile label also does not establish the amount, premium schedule, or future purchase rights. Check the policy and any guaranteed-insurability feature. A payor rider, if included, has separate conditions for protecting premium payments; it does not change whose life the base policy insures.

05 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Credit life connects protection to a specific debt

Marcus borrows money and is offered credit life insurance. Maryland defines it as insurance on a debtor's life connected to a specific loan or credit transaction. Individual credit life is issued to a debtor on a term plan. Group credit life uses a policy issued to the creditor covering debtors on a term plan, with the debtor receiving a certificate. The covered death benefit addresses the insured debt under the contract. It is different from a personally owned policy naming family members who may choose how to use proceeds. Credit life is not coverage for every reason a borrower might miss a payment.

06 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Use Maryland’s benefit and refund rules

Maryland generally limits the initial credit-life amount to the total repayable under the debt contract, with specific statutory exceptions. Determine the credit-life claim as of the debtor's death and use the actual benefit form. Net-payoff coverage includes the specified earned interest and past-due amounts. If a payable benefit exceeds what is due the creditor, the excess belongs to the person entitled under the rule, not automatically to the lender. Early termination requires the applicable refund or credit under approved rules and the minimum-refund provision. Refinancing does not justify silently stacking the old coverage and a new charge. APPLY / IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Go one step deeper.

Family plan is a coverage arrangement

A family plan may combine an adult base policy with term riders. It does not establish one universal family benefit, eligibility age or conversion deadline. The linked carrier brochure is an example of separate spouse and child benefits, not a Maryland-wide schedule.

Example: A child beneficiary on an adult policy is not thereby an insured child.

Two lives do not necessarily mean two payments

The first-death example pays once on the first covered death. The survivorship example pays once after the second death. Additional riders or continuation features must be stated before they change those answers.

Example: If the immediate need is money for a surviving spouse, a second-death base benefit alone does not arrive at that time.

Juvenile ownership and consent are separate questions

Identify the legal owner, insured and beneficiary. Apply the actual transfer provisions and Maryland’s minor-consent rules, including the parent/guardian provision in §12-202, rather than assuming the child is the owner or that consent rules vanish.

Example: A parent’s premium payment is not itself a transfer of the policy to the child.

Credit-life limits include specific exceptions

Section 13-106 generally caps initial credit life at the total amount repayable. It separately addresses an agricultural commitment of no more than one year and an educational commitment not yet advanced. Other applicable lending and insurance rules can also matter. Do not turn a simplified decreasing-debt example into a claim that every credit-life design must decrease identically.

Example: A general $12,000 total-repayable example does not support issuing $25,000 merely because the borrower wants extra family protection.

Determine who receives any excess

COMAR 31.13.01.25 determines the life claim as of the death date. Its net-payoff provision includes earned but unpaid interest, past-due principal installments and late charges. The rule directs any payable excess to the debtor, designated second beneficiary or estate, whichever is entitled.

Example: If an actual $10,000 payable benefit exceeds the $9,000 due to the creditor, the $1,000 excess goes to the entitled recipient under the applicable provision.

Early termination and delivery have distinct rules

Under §§13-107 and 13-112, apply the required termination and refund/credit provisions, approved formula and minimum-refund rule. Section 13-109 generally calls for the policy/certificate at the debt transaction; if absent, the specified signed application or notice must be delivered then, with the accepted policy/certificate delivered within 30 days of the debt date.

Example: A refinance that discharges the prior debt requires handling the prior coverage and applicable refund before issuing the replacement credit coverage.
