01 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Names, classes and priority answer different questions

Marcus names Elena as primary beneficiary and their adult daughter as contingent beneficiary. The primary beneficiary has first priority when eligible under the policy. The contingent beneficiary takes when the primary cannot, according to the stated succession rules. Naming an individual is different from designating a class, such as my children. Class wording must explain how shares pass if a member dies first. Per capita generally divides among the surviving members of the stated class. Per stirpes preserves a deceased member's branch for eligible descendants. Read the actual designation instead of treating every child and grandchild as an equal recipient.

Contingent

02 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Plan how a minor’s benefit will be managed

Elena wants insurance money to support a young child. A minor generally cannot receive and manage a large claim as an independent adult payee. An appropriate trust or custodial arrangement can provide a legally authorized person to manage the funds. A trust beneficiary is different from naming the insured's estate. Proceeds payable to the estate enter the estate administration process. Proceeds payable directly to an eligible named beneficiary ordinarily pass outside probate. The policy designation, trust documents and applicable law must work together. Simply writing a different wish in a will does not ordinarily replace the policy's beneficiary form.

03 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Revocable and irrevocable designations differ

A revocable beneficiary can ordinarily be changed through the owner's reserved contractual right. An irrevocable beneficiary has an interest that can restrict changes without the required consent. Those labels describe the ability to change rights, not whether the person is primary or contingent. Maryland permits insurer acceptance or endorsement requirements before a change binds the insurer. Check assignments and other valid restrictions too. A producer should help complete and confirm the correct request, rather than tell a client that a spoken instruction has already changed the insurer's records. Clear paperwork prevents avoidable disputes later.

04 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Family changes call for an actual review

After a divorce or annulment, review the policy designation, governing law, court orders and any agreement affecting insurance. Do not assume that a family-status change automatically rewrites every contract. A recent Maryland Insurance Administration example required payment to a former spouse who remained the named beneficiary of a Maryland life policy. The insurer could not import another state's automatic divorce rule. Wills have their own Maryland revocation provision, which is a different document rule. Some federal programs or contracts also have special requirements. Identify the applicable arrangement before advising who will receive the money.

05 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

A common disaster requires a survival rule

Suppose an insured and the primary beneficiary die in the same accident. The order and timing of death can affect which recipient takes. Read any common-disaster clause or required survival period. In a teaching policy, the primary beneficiary must survive the insured by thirty days. The primary dies two days later. The contingent beneficiary remains alive and is eligible to receive payment. Under that stated clause, the primary fails the required period and the contingent takes. Thirty days is this example's contract term, not a universal Maryland requirement. The written lesson distinguishes Maryland's current rule from a change effective in October twenty twenty-six.

06 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Facility of payment is a limited contract provision

A facility-of-payment provision can permit the insurer to pay an eligible person under specified circumstances when ordinary beneficiary payment cannot proceed as expected. Maryland's individual-life statute describes situations such as a minor beneficiary, a beneficiary who died first, or the other listed conditions. It identifies permitted recipients and limits the provision's operation. It does not let an insurer ignore a valid adult beneficiary merely because another person asks for the money. Distinguish this provision from an owner's beneficiary change and from a policy assignment. Start with the actual clause, the claimant's status and the applicable legal conditions.

APPLY / IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Go one step deeper.

Use an explicit class-distribution example

Assume a $300,000 benefit, two child branches, and a designation preserving each child’s branch per stirpes. One child is alive; the other died earlier leaving two living children. The living child receives $150,000; each grandchild in the other branch receives $75,000. Under an expressly stated surviving-children-only per-capita designation, the one surviving child would instead receive $300,000.

Example: The result comes from the specified designation. Do not assume all uses of class language have identical drafting.

Contingent does not mean simultaneous half-owner

A contingent beneficiary is next in the stated succession when the primary cannot take. If the sole eligible primary takes the full benefit under the designation, the contingent does not automatically receive half. Other share instructions, survival terms and legal restrictions can affect a real claim.

Example: A surviving eligible primary with a 100% designation ordinarily receives that stated share; the contingent is the backup.

Minor, trust and estate designations need different administration

A minor’s entitlement and the lawful person who receives or manages funds are separate issues. A properly arranged trust identifies a trustee and management terms. Naming the estate sends the benefit into estate administration; a direct eligible named beneficiary generally receives proceeds outside probate. Neither probate treatment nor a trust label alone guarantees a particular tax result.

Example: A parent should coordinate the designation with the chosen legal arrangement rather than assume a minor can immediately sign every required release.

Divorce does not supply one answer for every document

MIA Bulletin 26-22, dated September 10, 2026, describes a Maryland-sitused life policy paying the unchanged former-spouse beneficiary despite a divorce in Pennsylvania. The governing policy and law mattered. Maryland Estates and Trusts §4-105 separately revokes specified spousal will provisions following absolute divorce or annulment unless the will or decree provides otherwise. Do not transfer that will rule to every life designation.

Example: A question naming a federal plan, a specific agreement or an explicit contract provision requires examining those additional facts.

Maryland simultaneous-death law changes October 1, 2026

As of September 12, 2026, Courts and Judicial Proceedings §10-804 provides that when there is insufficient evidence the insured and beneficiary died other than simultaneously, life proceeds are distributed as though the insured survived the beneficiary; §10-805 recognizes a contrary contract distribution. Chapter 509 takes effect October 1, 2026 and generally introduces a 120-hour survival requirement with clear-and-convincing-evidence and specified exceptions, including applicable common-disaster language or an express different survival period.

Example: Use the event date and governing contract. The video’s stated 30-day contract scenario is not a claim that Maryland always requires 30 days or always requires 120 hours.

The facility-of-payment statute has specific conditions

Under §16-212(b), a permitted individual-life clause can address the named beneficiary’s failure to claim or surrender proof within a policy period of at least 30 days after death, an estate beneficiary, a minor, a beneficiary who predeceased the insured, or one unable legally to give a valid release. Permitted recipients include the estate, listed relatives or a person equitably entitled under the statute. It is a policy permission subject to conditions, not an unrestricted command to pay any relative.

Example: A valid adult primary beneficiary promptly submitting the required claim does not lose priority simply because another relative prefers payment.
