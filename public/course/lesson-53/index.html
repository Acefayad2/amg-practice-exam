01 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

An IRA is an individual tax arrangement

Marcus wants to save part of his earnings in a traditional individual retirement arrangement, usually called an I R A. It can be established through an eligible financial institution, including an insurance company. The account is individually owned, even when spouses use a joint tax return. Eligibility for a regular contribution generally requires qualifying compensation, such as wages or net self-employment income. A pension check or investment interest is not automatically that compensation. There is no upper age cutoff for regular contributions under current law, but the other requirements remain. A spousal contribution can be possible on a joint return using qualifying combined compensation; it still goes into a separate individual arrangement.

02 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Use the tax year and the combined limit

For tax year twenty twenty-six, the general combined limit for regular traditional and Roth I R A contributions is seven thousand five hundred dollars, or qualifying compensation if that is less. Someone age fifty or older can have an additional eleven hundred dollar catch-up, making the general dollar limit eighty-six hundred dollars. Suppose Marcus is forty-five and has only four thousand dollars of qualifying compensation, with no spousal rule. His regular contribution limit is four thousand dollars. If he has enough compensation and contributes three thousand dollars to a traditional account, that generally leaves forty-five hundred dollars of the combined annual limit, before checking Roth income restrictions.

03 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Being allowed to contribute does not establish a deduction

A traditional I R A contribution may be fully deductible, partly deductible or nondeductible. Workplace retirement coverage, filing status and modified adjusted gross income affect the deduction rules. Being covered by an employer plan does not by itself prohibit a regular traditional contribution. It can instead limit the deduction. If neither spouse is covered by a workplace plan, those income phaseouts for deducting contributions do not apply, although contribution limits still do. A permitted nondeductible contribution must be tracked as basis, generally using Form eighty-six oh six. Keep that record so after-tax money is not taxed again. Contribution eligibility and deduction eligibility are separate exam questions.

04 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Tax deferral is followed by distribution rules

Growth in a traditional I R A generally is not taxed annually while it stays inside the arrangement. If it contains only deductible contributions and untaxed earnings, an ordinary distribution generally is fully taxable income. Buying an annuity inside that account does not create an after-tax investment that never existed. If nondeductible amounts are present, the required allocation can make distributions partly taxable; the owner cannot simply choose to withdraw only basis from one account. A taxable six thousand dollar distribution at age forty-five, with no exception, generally also produces six hundred dollars of additional federal tax. An exception to that additional tax does not automatically remove ordinary income tax.

05 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Required distributions depend on the applicable age

Consider an owner who turns seventy-three during twenty twenty-six. The first traditional I R A required minimum distribution is for that year and generally must be taken by April first of twenty twenty-seven. The next year's required distribution is due by December thirty-first of twenty twenty-seven, so delaying the first payment can put both in one tax year. Continuing to work does not postpone this traditional account requirement. Use the applicable birth-year rule rather than memorizing age seventy and one half from old material. Current law also schedules age seventy-five for younger cohorts. A required minimum distribution is a minimum withdrawal requirement, not an instruction to surrender every annuity or spend the entire account.

06 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

A beneficiary designation does not erase the tax rules

A surviving spouse can have choices that a nonspouse beneficiary does not, including an eligible election to treat an inherited account as their own. Many adult nonspouse designated beneficiaries instead face a ten-year distribution deadline. Do not assume that means no earlier annual payments: the owner's required beginning date and beneficiary category matter. For an owner dying after the required beginning date, annual beneficiary distributions generally continue as well as the applicable tenth-year deadline. A beneficiary's receipt of traditional account earnings is not automatically excluded life insurance proceeds. Identify the owner's death date, remaining basis, beneficiary relationship and applicable category before selecting a payout rule.

APPLY / IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Go one step deeper.

2026 figures are dated teaching facts

The 2026 regular traditional/Roth IRA combined dollar limit is $7,500, plus a $1,100 catch-up at age 50 or older, subject to qualifying compensation and other eligibility limits. Rollovers are not regular contributions under that dollar limit. For most taxpayers, a regular contribution for a tax year is due by the return due date without extensions.

Example: A 45-year-old with $4,000 qualifying compensation and no spousal rule is limited to $4,000 regular contributions, even with $20,000 in savings.

Deduction and contribution restrictions are different

A covered single taxpayer’s traditional IRA deduction phases out over $81,000–$91,000 modified AGI for 2026. For joint filers when the contributor is covered, the range is $129,000–$149,000. When the contributor is not covered but the spouse is, the applicable joint-filer range is $242,000–$252,000. Married-separate rules require their own facts. These deduction ranges do not themselves prohibit a permitted nondeductible traditional contribution.

Example: Maintain Form 8606 records for nondeductible contributions.

After-tax basis is not selected account by account

Traditional IRA distribution calculations generally aggregate the owner’s traditional, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs as required by Form 8606. The calculation includes year-end values, distributions and applicable adjustments. A separate account labeled nondeductible does not necessarily isolate a tax-free withdrawal. An all-pretax IRA annuity has no after-tax investment recovery merely because it makes periodic payments.

Example: The annuity exclusion example in Part 52 expressly used a different, nonqualified arrangement with after-tax investment.

Additional-tax exceptions require their own conditions

IRA exceptions can include qualifying higher education, a first-home distribution within its $10,000 lifetime limit, death, qualifying disability and specified substantially equal periodic payments. Other statutory exceptions exist. Age 59½ can remove the usual early-distribution additional tax while ordinary tax remains. A SIMPLE IRA within its initial two-year period can have a different rate, addressed in the plan lesson.

Example: Do not transfer an IRA-only education exception to a personal nonqualified annuity.

RMD ages and deadlines must match the cohort

The video uses a person born in 1953, reaching 73 in 2026. The later statutory age-75 schedule applies to people born in 1960 or later. Older cohorts retain their applicable earlier rules. Traditional IRA owners do not receive the still-employed postponement that can apply to some employer plans. RMD amounts are not eligible for rollover.

Example: Missing an RMD can trigger a separate excise tax; do not confuse it with the 10% early-distribution tax.

Inherited arrangements need classification

Eligible designated beneficiaries include a surviving spouse, the owner’s minor child, a qualifying disabled or chronically ill individual, or an individual no more than ten years younger than the owner. Other designated individuals commonly face the ten-year rule. When the owner dies before the required beginning date and that rule applies, no annual distribution is generally required before the final year. When the owner dies on or after that date, annual distributions generally apply as well. Estate and qualifying trust rules differ.

Example: An inherited traditional IRA retains its income-tax character; beneficiary naming does not turn pretax funds into excluded life death proceeds.
