01 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

SEP: employer contributions to individual arrangements

Marcus operates a small business and considers a simplified employee pension, usually called a SEP. A regular SEP uses employer contributions to individual retirement arrangements established for eligible workers. The employer can decide whether to contribute for a year, but cannot ignore required employee coverage or the applicable allocation rules when making contributions. Contributions belong to the employee immediately; a SEP does not use a waiting period for ownership of those deposits. A regular SEP contribution is not an employee salary deferral under a new four-oh-one-k plan. The SEP is the employer arrangement, while the individual account and its investments hold the contributed funds. Keep those layers distinct when identifying the product on an exam.

02 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Keogh identifies self-employed plan history

A question may call a retirement plan for a self-employed person a Keogh or H R ten plan. These are older names associated with qualified retirement plans for self-employed individuals and unincorporated businesses. They do not identify one unique insurance policy or one guaranteed investment return. A self-employed business can use different qualified designs, including defined contribution or defined benefit arrangements. The plan still must satisfy its applicable eligibility, contribution and other qualification requirements. If the business has eligible common-law employees, the owner cannot assume those workers disappear from the analysis. First identify the business and the plan structure, then determine contributions and benefits under that structure rather than choosing an answer from the name alone.

03 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Profit sharing and 401(k) describe different features

In a profit-sharing plan, the employer generally has discretion over how much to contribute, subject to the plan and legal requirements. The name does not mean the business must show a profit before any contribution is possible. A four-oh-one-k feature allows employees to elect salary deferrals, and the employer may also make matching or other permitted contributions. A plan can combine these features. Do not confuse employer discretion in a profit-sharing contribution with an employee's salary-reduction election. If a question describes Elena choosing to defer part of her wages, that fact points to the employee deferral feature. If it describes the employer deciding an annual contribution under a qualifying formula, identify the employer contribution being discussed.

04 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

SIMPLE IRA: small-employer saving with employer contributions

A SIMPLE individual retirement account plan is designed for eligible small employers, generally those with one hundred or fewer qualifying employees, and ordinarily no other retirement plan. Employees may make salary-reduction contributions, and the employer must make the required contribution under the selected permitted formula. Common standard formulas use a match up to three percent of compensation or a two percent nonelective contribution for eligible employees. Current rules allow additional variations for specified employers and plan elections, so those percentages are not a universal statement of every SIMPLE arrangement. All SIMPLE account contributions are immediately vested. Distinguish a required employer contribution from a promise that every worker will choose to defer part of their own wages.

05 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

403(b): eligible schools and tax-exempt organizations

Rosa works for a public school offering a four-oh-three-b retirement plan. These arrangements also serve eligible tax-exempt organizations, including qualifying organizations under section five-oh-one-c-three. The term tax-sheltered annuity appears in study materials, but permitted funding is not limited to an insurance annuity contract. A qualifying custodial account invested in mutual funds can also be used, and church retirement income accounts have their own permitted structure. Employee salary reductions commonly fund the plan, and employer contributions may also be available. A regular private for-profit employer does not become an eligible four-oh-three-b sponsor merely by choosing an annuity investment. Identify the sponsoring organization before applying the plan label.

06 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Match the facts before comparing the investments

Put the cases together. Marcus's employer-funded individual retirement arrangement points toward a SEP. Elena's elective salary deferrals point toward a four-oh-one-k feature. Rosa's eligible public-school plan points toward four-oh-three-b. A qualifying small employer combining employee salary reductions with required employer contributions may use SIMPLE. The same annuity investment could appear in more than one arrangement without making their legal rules identical. Also identify pretax and Roth treatment separately. Current law permits Roth options in specified SEP and SIMPLE arrangements; old claims that they can never offer Roth treatment are outdated. Before answering, identify the sponsor, contributor, ownership rule, plan requirements and tax character actually stated in the question.

APPLY / IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Go one step deeper.

SEP and an old SARSEP are different

A regular SEP uses employer plan contributions. Salary-reduction SEPs have their own historical rules: new SARSEPs could not be established after 1996, though qualifying existing arrangements can continue. Employees may separately have ordinary IRA contribution rights if eligible.

Example: Do not describe a new regular SEP as an unrestricted salary-deferral plan.

Profit-sharing contributions do not require actual current profits

The plan’s name does not impose a profits-only funding restriction. Employer contributions still follow the document, allocation, nondiscrimination and deduction/contribution rules. A 401(k) feature can be part of a profit-sharing arrangement.

Example: Discretion about whether to contribute is different from permission to allocate arbitrarily.

SIMPLE details require the current plan facts

The general employer-size test counts employees receiving at least $5,000 in the preceding year, with specified transition and other exceptions. Standard 3% matching or 2% nonelective designs are not the only current rules: enhanced-limit elections for certain employers can require 4% matching or 3% nonelective contributions. Consult current IRS guidance for the actual year and plan.

Example: The ordinary early-distribution additional tax can increase from 10% to 25% within the first two years of SIMPLE participation when no exception applies.

Plan family does not determine a single investment

403(b) funding can include an annuity, a qualifying mutual-fund custodial account or a qualifying church retirement income account. Eligibility is determined by the sponsor and legal rules, not simply by purchasing an annuity.

Example: A private for-profit employer cannot obtain 403(b) eligibility just by using the words tax-sheltered annuity.

Current Roth options prevent a blanket all-pretax rule

SECURE 2.0 permits Roth SEP and Roth SIMPLE contributions under the applicable elections and requirements. Availability depends on the arrangement. Do not assume the tax character from the SEP or SIMPLE label alone.

Example: Qualified distribution and contribution-income rules must still be applied to the actual Roth funds.
