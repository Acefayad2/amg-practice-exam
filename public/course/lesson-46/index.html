01 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Pure life pays while the covered person lives

David compares annuity income options using the same amount of money. Pure life, also called straight life or life only, pays for his covered lifetime and stops at his death, without an added refund or guaranteed payment period. If he dies soon after income begins, a beneficiary does not automatically receive the amount he originally paid. If he lives much longer, lifetime payments continue under the promise. Compared with otherwise similar options that add survivor or refund guarantees, pure life generally provides a larger initial periodic payment. That comparison assumes the same funding and comparable contract terms. A larger check comes with a different protection pattern.

02 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Life with a certain period protects two possibilities

Now assume David chooses life income with ten years certain. If he dies after exactly four years of payments, the remaining six years of guaranteed payments continue to the designated recipient under the option. If he is still living after ten years, his life payments continue. The ten-year period is a minimum payment guarantee, not a limit on his life income. Compare that with a ten-year period-certain-only option. That option pays for the stated ten years and ends even if David remains alive. The words life with change the result. Read whether the promise lasts for a lifetime, a defined period, or the longer of those two.

03 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Refund options protect a stated minimum total

A life-with-refund option pays lifetime income and adds a stated minimum-total protection if the annuitant dies early. Assume one hundred thousand dollars is the contract's refundable purchase amount and thirty thousand dollars has been paid when David dies. With no other adjustment, seventy thousand dollars remains under that refund promise. A cash-refund option pays that amount in one sum. An installment-refund option continues the stated installments until the required total has been paid. Those beneficiary installments do not automatically last for the beneficiary's lifetime. If David instead lives long enough to receive more than the purchase amount, his life income still continues under the contract.

04 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

For two lives, check the survivor’s percentage

Rosa and David want income to continue while either of them lives. A joint-and-survivor option can provide that protection. Suppose the stated payment is two thousand dollars each month while both live, with seventy-five percent continuing to the survivor. After the triggering death, the survivor receives fifteen hundred dollars monthly under those assumptions. A full-survivor option would continue the stated full amount; other percentages produce different results. The words joint or two lives alone do not establish the continuation amount. Read whose death triggers a reduction and whether payments continue to the last survivor. That is different from an arrangement expressly payable only while both people remain alive.

05 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Fixed period and fixed amount fix different things

A fixed-period option sets the number of payment periods, and the available funds and stated interest assumptions determine the periodic amount. A fixed-amount option instead sets the payment size, with the available funds and interest determining how long payments can last. In a simplified zero-interest example, sixty thousand dollars paid at one thousand dollars per month lasts sixty months. Changing the amount to fifteen hundred dollars shortens that to forty months. Those are teaching calculations, not lifetime guarantees or insurer quotes. A client who needs lifelong income must not mistake a chosen monthly withdrawal amount for a promise that money can never run out.

06 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Match the guarantee to the household’s priority

Rosa asks whether the highest available initial payment is always the best choice. It is not. Compare the need for income during one or two lifetimes, a minimum amount for beneficiaries, available cash elsewhere and the effect of inflation. A fixed dollar payment can buy less as prices rise, even when every promised payment arrives. The choice also depends on the specific contract, because income elections and later access may be restricted. For an exam, identify what is guaranteed first: a life, a number of years, a minimum total or a payment size. Then follow the stated death or survival event to determine which payments stop, continue or change.

APPLY / IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Go one step deeper.

Life only transfers longevity risk without an added estate refund

Pure life generally offers a larger initial periodic amount than an otherwise comparable option that adds a guaranteed period, refund or survivor protection. Compare the same funding, age and other relevant assumptions; do not compare unrelated insurer quotes as if only the option changed.

Example: An early death can leave no further payment under a life-only option.

A minimum period does not cap the life promise

Life with ten years certain pays for life, with the stated ten-year minimum. Ten years certain without a life promise pays for exactly that period. A beneficiary receives only the continuation the selected option provides.

Example: After four completed years, a ten-year guarantee has six years remaining.

Cash and installment refund use different payment methods

Under the stated refund design, subtract amounts already paid from the refundable purchase amount. Cash refund pays the remaining amount at once; installment refund continues the scheduled payments to satisfy the required total. The contract may specify its calculation and adjustments.

Example: The example’s remaining $70,000 is not an additional $100,000 refund.

Read a two-life promise through both deaths

A joint-and-survivor election can continue a stated percentage while the survivor lives. Do not assume the percentage is 100%. An option expressly payable only while both remain alive would instead end at the first death; use the provided terms.

Example: Seventy-five percent of $2,000 is $1,500.

A fixed period controls time; a fixed amount controls size

For a fixed period, the available amount and applicable interest determine each payment over the chosen duration. For a fixed amount, the funds and interest determine the duration. The zero-interest classroom examples exclude fees and adjustments and are not product quotes.

Example: Raising a $1,000 payment to $1,500 uses the stated funds faster.

Nominal guarantees and purchasing power are different

A guaranteed level-dollar payment can lose purchasing power as prices increase. Compare any available increasing-income provision and its initial-payment tradeoff under the actual contract.

Example: Receiving the promised $1,500 does not guarantee that it buys the same basket of goods forever.
