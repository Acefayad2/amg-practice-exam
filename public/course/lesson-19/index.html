01 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Begin with the family, then the policy

Marcus and Elena sit down with their household budget. They want the children to stay in their home and finish school if either parent dies. The first step is fact gathering: income, expenses, debts, dependents, existing coverage, savings, goals and an affordable premium. Include unpaid work such as childcare and household management. Losing that support can create a replacement cost even without losing a paycheck. The agent should understand who depends on each person and for how long. A product name or an income multiplier cannot answer those questions by itself.

02 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Two methods answer different questions

The human-life-value approach looks at the financial value of future earnings or contributions. A detailed estimate uses expected working years and present-value assumptions, with appropriate adjustments for amounts not supporting survivors. The needs approach starts with the specific money the survivors would require, then subtracts resources available to meet those needs. These methods can produce different estimates because their assumptions differ. Neither number measures a person's worth. The purpose is to estimate a financial exposure. Make the assumptions visible and review them as the household's circumstances change.

03 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Separate immediate needs from ongoing income

The family identifies one hundred fifty thousand dollars for a mortgage payoff, thirty thousand for final expenses and other debts, and forty thousand for education. Those stated lump-sum needs total two hundred twenty thousand dollars. They also estimate an ongoing income shortfall of three thousand dollars per month for ten years. In a deliberately simplified calculation with no interest or inflation, that income need is three hundred sixty thousand dollars. Keep the categories separate. If the mortgage is paid off, do not also include the same mortgage payments in the future monthly shortfall.

04 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Subtract resources that are truly available

The simplified needs total is five hundred eighty thousand dollars. The family identifies eighty thousand dollars of savings actually available for these needs and one hundred thousand dollars of existing death protection assumed to remain in force. Subtracting both leaves a four hundred thousand dollar gap. This is a teaching example, not a quote or a recommendation. Verify whether employer coverage continues, whether assets have other commitments and whether a survivor can realistically earn the assumed income. A resource should not be counted twice or treated as immediately available when access is restricted.

05 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Protection can preserve an estate

A family may own a valuable home or business but have little ready cash. Liquidity means money is available to meet obligations without a forced sale. A life benefit may help with final expenses, debts or applicable estate costs and help preserve assets for survivors. Estate planning also considers the owner, beneficiary and legal arrangement; it is broader than simply buying a policy. Some permanent policies offer cash accumulation during life, subject to their contract, charges and funding. Keep that feature distinct from the death benefit and from money guaranteed to be immediately accessible.

06 / UNDERSTAND THE REASON

Government benefits are a verified resource

Social Security survivor benefits may help eligible spouses, children or dependent parents based on the deceased worker's record. They do not automatically replace the household's full income. Eligibility, ages, work history, earnings and family maximums can affect payment. A child's benefit and a surviving spouse's caregiving benefit can end at different times, creating an income gap. Use an actual benefit estimate and the expected payment period rather than assuming an amount. Then review the full plan: immediate needs, ongoing support, available resources, affordable coverage and the changes that should trigger a new review.

APPLY / IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Go one step deeper.

A simple human-life-value calculation needs explicit assumptions

Assume a person contributes $40,000 annually to the household for twenty more years. With an expressly assumed zero discount rate and no growth, the simplified total is $800,000. A positive discount rate reduces the present value of the same future payments; changes to income growth, taxes, personal consumption and timing also change the estimate.

Example: Do not call twenty times gross salary a fully individualized present-value calculation.

The calculation deliberately leaves out real-world variables

The $400,000 illustration assumes no inflation, no investment return, a constant ten-year income shortfall, $180,000 of available resources and no overlap between the mortgage payoff and income need. A real analysis considers the timing and reliability of each amount and the cost of maintaining the selected coverage.

Example: If the $80,000 savings must remain an emergency reserve, it should not also be fully assigned to reduce this protection gap.

Household services have financial value

Childcare, transportation and household work may need paid replacements after a caregiver dies. Fact gathering should include those services and the surviving adult’s ability to continue paid work.

Example: A parent with no paid employment can still create a substantial financial exposure for the household.

Cash accumulation and estate liquidity are different uses

Cash accumulation concerns accessible policy values during life, subject to product terms. Estate liquidity concerns having funds when obligations arise after death. Estate conservation concerns preserving assets rather than selling them under pressure. A personally owned mortgage-protection life policy can provide a beneficiary funds to address a mortgage, while credit life has a debt-linked structure taught separately.

Example: A house may be valuable but illiquid; an available death benefit can address a cash need without itself changing the mortgage contract.

Recognize the survivor-benefit timing gap

Under the stated general SSA rules, a surviving spouse may qualify by caring for the deceased worker’s qualifying child under sixteen or with a disability. An unmarried child may qualify while under eighteen, or at eighteen to nineteen in full-time elementary or secondary school, with separate disability and other exceptions. A caregiving benefit may therefore end before an ordinary age-based widow or widower benefit begins.

Example: A spouse who is forty-five when the youngest qualifying child turns sixteen should not assume continuous spouse benefits until retirement age. Check the actual facts and exceptions with SSA.

Other survivor categories and limits matter

An eligible surviving spouse may receive age-based benefits from sixty, or from fifty with a qualifying disability; dependent parents may qualify from sixty-two. Work-record, relationship and other conditions apply. Children generally receive 75% of the worker’s benefit, subject to the family maximum. Survivor and other Social Security benefits are not simply added together.

Example: Do not subtract a full assumed Social Security amount from every family’s insurance need without verifying eligibility and timing.
